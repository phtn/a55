Of Intelligence & Uncertainties

True intelligence consists not in the mere acquisition of knowledge, nor in the ardent certainty with which we hold ever strongly, the opinions we've under explored and half examined. Its truer seat lies in a sober apprehension of the boundaries of our judgment: in discerning how far conviction may guide before it degenerates into delusion, and in possessing the candor to know where one must firmly persist, and where wisdom itself demands concession and navigates differing viewpoints.

For the mind most liable to error is not the ignorant mind, but that which has mistaken partial truth for universal law. We are seldom ruined by the absence of thought; we are ruined by the excess of certainty with which we enthrone a fragment of thought above the whole. Every age has furnished examples of individuals who, having perceived one principle clearly, became thereafter blind to all others, until what began as insight hardened gradually into bigotry.

It is therefore among the highest duties of an intellectual being to preserve within himself a perpetual tribunal of self-examination; to submit even his dearest principles to scrutiny, and to endure, without resentment, the possibility that he may stand corrected. For there is a species of cowardice concealed beneath obstinacy, as there is a species of courage in the admission of doubt. The former armed with conviction; the latter wears uncertainty, the value of truth and integrity.

Thus the wisest man is not he who never wavers, but he who knows that firmness and humility are affinities of such stability. He stands where reason compels him to stand. Never with such conceit as to believe the ground beneath him would forever last, for the earth itself shifts as fast as past.
